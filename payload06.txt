        <div><div class="kicker">Module 8</div><h3>Quantum basics: why post-quantum cryptography became necessary</h3></div>
        <p>Post-quantum cryptography is about making cryptographic algorithms safe against the possible development of large-scale quantum computers. The lecture focuses mainly on the threat to <strong>asymmetric cryptography</strong>.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="grid2">
        <div class="card" data-pop="quantum"><h4>Quantum computing</h4><p>Quantum computing represents information using physical principles from quantum mechanics. It is not just a faster normal computer; it uses a different computational model.</p></div>
        <div class="card" data-pop="qubit"><h4>Qubit</h4><p>A qubit is the quantum analogue of a bit. Unlike a classical bit, it can exist in a <strong>superposition</strong> of states before measurement.</p></div>
      </div>
      <div class="accordion" style="margin-top:14px">
        <div class="acc"><button><span>Superposition and entanglement in simple words</span><span>+</span></button><div class="inner"><p><strong>Superposition</strong> means a qubit can be in a mixture of 0 and 1 until measured. <strong>Entanglement</strong> means qubits can become linked so that the combined state has structure you cannot explain by treating each qubit separately. These properties help explain why some problems can be attacked very differently on a quantum computer.</p></div></div>

      <div class="section-head">
        <div><div class="kicker">Module 9</div><h3>Quantum risk: Shor, Grover, cryptoperiod and vulnerable systems</h3></div>
        <p>The lecture distinguishes between two major quantum impacts. <strong>Shor’s algorithm</strong> threatens public-key systems based on factoring or discrete logarithms. <strong>Grover’s algorithm</strong> weakens brute-force resistance for symmetric crypto and hashes, but in a more manageable way.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="grid2">
        <div class="card" data-pop="shor"><h4>Shor’s algorithm</h4><p>Targets integer factorisation and discrete logarithms efficiently. That is why RSA, DH, DSA, ECDH and ECDSA-style systems are considered vulnerable.</p><div class="quick">Big conclusion: many current public-key foundations need replacement, not just bigger keys.</div></div>
        <div class="card" data-pop="grover"><h4>Grover’s algorithm</h4><p>Provides a square-root speed-up for search. The lecture’s practical intuition is that a 128-bit symmetric key may need to move toward 256-bit strength, and hash lengths may also need to double for comparable margin.</p><div class="quick">Big conclusion: symmetric cryptography survives better than asymmetric cryptography.</div></div>
      </div>
