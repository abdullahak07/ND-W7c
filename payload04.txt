ong>minimal resource demands</strong> on the host system while still providing useful security.</p>
          <div class="quick">Easy memory line: normal crypto assumes comfort; lightweight crypto survives under pressure.</div>
        </div>
        <div class="card">
          <h4>Why we need it</h4>
          <p>Many IoT nodes, RFID tags, small sensors and controllers have limited CPU power, limited RAM/ROM, low data rates and very little energy. A heavy solution may simply not fit or may drain the device too quickly.</p>
        </div>
      </div>
      <div class="accordion" style="margin-top:14px">
        <div class="acc open"><button><span>Click to view fuller explanation</span><span>−</span></button><div class="inner"><p>The word “lightweight” does <strong>not</strong> mean weak or insecure. It means the design is careful about resource usage. The lecture’s big idea is that a good design balances <strong>security, cost and performance</strong>. If we blindly choose the strongest and biggest option, small devices may become unusable. If we choose the cheapest or fastest option, security may collapse. Lightweight crypto tries to stay secure enough while fitting inside real-world limits.</p></div></div>

        <div><div class="kicker">Module 2</div><h3>Embedded systems, microcontrollers and deeply embedded devices</h3></div>
        <p>An <strong>embedded system</strong> is a computer built into a product for a specific function. It is not a general-purpose laptop or desktop. A <strong>microcontroller</strong> is often the core chip inside these systems.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="grid3">
        <div class="card" data-pop="embedded"><h4>Embedded system</h4><p>Electronics and software inside a product that performs a specific job, such as control, sensing, timing or communication.</p><span class="mini-tag">specific function</span><span class="mini-tag">inside a product</span></div>
        <div class="card" data-pop="microcontroller"><h4>Microcontroller</h4><p>A “computer on a chip” that typically includes a processor, ROM/flash, RAM, a clock and I/O controllers.</p><span class="mini-tag">compact</span><span class="mini-tag">energy efficient</span></div>
        <div class="card" data-pop="deeply"><h4>Deeply embedded system</h4><p>Very small, hard to observe, often with no direct user interaction and extremely limited resources.</p><span class="mini-tag">hidden</span><span class="mini-tag">very constrained</span></div>
      </div>
      <div class="accordion" style="margin-top:14px">

        <div class="acc"><button><span>Why this matters for security</span><span>+</span></button><div class="inner"><p>Because these devices are often small, cheap and everywhere, security has to be compact and efficient.     <section id="m3" class="reveal">
      <div class="section-head">
        <div><div class="kicker">Module 3</div><h3>Constrained devices and the limits designers must respect</h3></div>
        <p>A constrained device has limited memory, limited processing power and often a low-data-rate radio. The lecture also discusses device classes and the main categories of constraints that shape lightweight cryptographic design.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="grid3">
        <div class="card" data-pop="class0"><h4>Class 0</h4><p>Extremely constrained motes or “smart dust.” Usually preconfigured, minimally manageable and too limited for traditional security management.</p></div>
        <div class="card" data-pop="class1"><h4>Class 1</h4><p>Still constrained, but capable of participating in meaningful communication using lightweight protocol stacks designed for such nodes.</p></div>
        <div class="card" data-pop="class2"><h4>Class 2</h4><p>Less constrained and more capable, but still far below normal laptops/servers, so low-power and efficient security remains important.</p></div>
      </div>
      <div class="grid3" style="margin-top:14px">
        <div class="card" data-pop="chip"><h4>Chip area</h4><p>Important when crypto is implemented in hardware. Tiny devices cannot spend much silicon area on security logic.</p></div>
        <div class="card" data-pop="energy"><h4>Energy</h4><p>Battery-powered or harvested-energy devices need low-power computation and communication.</p></div>
        <div class="card" data-pop="memory"><h4>Code + RAM</h4><p>Program code and working memory must stay small.</p></div>
        <div class="card" data-pop="rate"><h4>Transmission rate</h4><p>Links may be slow, so the amount of security data sent over the network must be small.</p></div>
        <div class="card" data-pop="exec"><h4>Execution time</h4><p>Some devices, such as RFID tags and contactless cards, only have a short time window to respond.</p></div>
        <div class="card" data-pop="radio"><h4>Typical radio</h4><p>Many devices use low-power links such as IEEE 802.15.4, which further limits how much overhead security can introduce.</p></div>
      </div>
      <div class="quick">Easy memory line: the security must fit the device — in chip area, energy, memory, bandwidth and time.</div>
